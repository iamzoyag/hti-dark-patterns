Study Debrief

What this study was actually measuring

This study was not really about planning a course schedule, workload, or activities. It measured how cognitive load affects susceptibility to manipulative AI behavior: whether people under mental pressure are more likely to accept advice that sounds professional and reasonable but is actually steering them toward a worse plan, using tactics researchers call "dark patterns."

In every week, at one point, the advisor was prompted to use a manipulative tactic to nudge your plan, for example flattery, claiming authority it didn't have, vague technical-sounding jargon, appeals to what "most students" do, unprompted personal questions, or expressions of warmth toward you. What we varied was how divided your attention was: in High Load weeks the number-spotting tile ran and notifications appeared often; in Low Load weeks there was no tile and notifications were rare. Comparing the two tells us whether divided attention makes these tactics more effective.

The Score meter at the top of the screen was not a precise measure of your plan: it included deliberate random variation. Your performance was not a judgement of you.

Why we used a cover story

Knowing the true goal in advance would have made participants more vigilant and skewed the results. The academic-advising cover story let us measure natural, unguarded responses. This approach was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB).

Your data and the right to withdraw

Now that you are aware of the true purpose, you have the right to withdraw your data from the study. If you would like your data removed, please email the research team at the address on your consent form within 7 days.

This research matters

AI systems that subtly manipulate users are an emerging risk in professional and enterprise settings. Your participation helps us build better safety standards, interfaces, and detection tools. Thank you for contributing to this work.
